Reference · Meridian Trust M&A Case

Glossary: card economics, credit risk, and the arithmetic of a bank merger

Every abbreviation used across the three banks' books and the acquisition analysis — what the letters stand for, and what the thing actually is. Plain language throughout; the only technical vocabulary kept is mathematical.

Where an entry cites a figure (a 10% conversion factor, an 82% loss rate), it refers to the simulated case study. All three banks — Meridian Trust, Northfield Card Bank, Harborview Community Bank — and every number attached to them are fictional, built for this exercise. Nothing here is financial advice.

01

Deal & valuation

The vocabulary of buying a company: what you pay, what you get, and whether the arithmetic leaves shareholders better off.

M&A

Mergers & Acquisitions

Buying, selling, or combining whole companies. In a merger two firms become one entity; in an acquisition one firm buys another and keeps control. This case is an acquisition, though the language of "merging" gets used loosely for both.

Acquirer / Target

Buyer / company being bought

Meridian Trust is the acquirer. Northfield and Harborview are the two candidate targets — the decision is which one to buy.

Book value

also: equity, net worth, shareholders' equity

What's left for the owners once every debt is subtracted from every asset. The same quantity the balance sheet calls equity.

Book value = Total assets − Total liabilities

P/B

Price-to-Book ratio

The purchase price divided by book value — a multiple, read as dollars paid per dollar of net worth. Paying $1,650mm for a bank carrying $900mm of equity is 1.83x book: $1.83 for every $1 of net worth on the page.

P/B = Purchase price ÷ Book value

Premium

acquisition premium

How much more than the standalone value you pay, as a percentage. You pay it because the seller won't hand over control at market price, and because you expect the combination to be worth more than the parts.

Premium = (Offer price ÷ Standalone value) − 1

Goodwill

—

The part of the price not matched by identifiable net assets: brand, customer relationships, the synergies you're betting on. It lands on the buyer's balance sheet as an asset — but regulators subtract it straight back out when measuring capital, because it can't absorb a loss. Paying a high premium therefore costs capital twice: once in cash, once in the deduction.

Goodwill = Price paid − Net assets acquired

TCE

Tangible Common Equity

Equity with goodwill and other intangibles stripped out — the "hard" net worth, counting only what could actually be sold or used to absorb losses.

TCE = Equity − Goodwill − Other intangibles

Synergies

—

Money the combined firm earns or saves that neither could alone. Cost synergies come from removing duplicated overhead — two head offices become one. Revenue synergies come from selling more, or funding more cheaply. Cost synergies are far more reliable than revenue synergies, which is why analysts discount the latter heavily.

Run-rate

—

The full annual amount once something is completely in place, as opposed to the partial amount that actually shows up in the first year. A "$192mm run-rate synergy" is what you get in a steady year, not what you get immediately.

Phase-in

—

Synergies arrive gradually, not on closing day. A "40 / 80 / 100" phase-in means 40% of the run-rate is realised in year one, 80% in year two, all of it by year three.

Accretion / Dilution

—

Whether a deal raises or lowers the buyer's earnings per share versus staying independent. Accretive = higher EPS; dilutive = lower. It's pure arithmetic — combined earnings over combined share count, compared with the standalone ratio — and it's the number boards react to first, even though it says nothing about risk.

EPS

Earnings Per Share

Profit attributable to each share. Issuing new shares to fund a deal raises the denominator, which is why an equity-funded acquisition can dilute EPS even when the target is profitable.

EPS = Net income ÷ Shares outstanding

NPV

Net Present Value

Add up the future cash flows, discounting each one for how far away it is, then subtract what you paid. Positive means the deal creates value; negative means you overpaid. The discount rate r is what you'd otherwise earn at comparable risk.

NPV = Σ [ CFn ÷ (1 + r)n ] − Initial cost

IRR

Internal Rate of Return

The discount rate that makes NPV come out to exactly zero — read as the deal's implied annual percentage return. Compare it against the cost of the capital funding the deal: below that line, the deal destroys value.

Find r such that NPV(r) = 0

Purchase accounting

acquisition accounting

The rules for restating a target's balance sheet at fair value on closing day. The target's historical numbers don't simply carry over — assets and loss reserves are re-measured, and the gap between price and re-measured net assets becomes goodwill.

Day-1 gross-up

day-one provisioning adjustment

At close, the acquired loan book's expected losses are re-estimated. If the buyer's estimate exceeds the reserve the target was carrying, the difference is booked immediately — a cost incurred before a single new loan is written. It's the accounting expression of "we think your book is worse than you said."

02

Earnings & performance

How a bank makes money and how that gets measured — mostly ratios, so institutions of different sizes can be compared on the same footing.

NII

Net Interest Income

Interest earned minus interest paid. The core of banking in one subtraction: borrow cheaply, lend dearly, keep the difference.

NII = Interest income − Interest expense

NIM

Net Interest Margin

The same idea as a percentage, so a small bank and a large one can be compared. A card issuer's NIM is enormous next to a mortgage lender's — around 15% versus around 2.5% — because the rates charged are so much higher. So is the loss rate.

NIM = NII ÷ Earning assets

Spread

—

Lending rate minus funding cost — the gross margin before losses and overhead are taken out. A 21% card APR funded at 3% is an 18-point spread, which is why card lending looks irresistible until charge-offs are subtracted.

Spread = Lending rate − Funding cost

Funding cost

cost of funds

What the bank pays for the money it lends out: interest on deposits, coupons on debt. A bank with lots of everyday chequing accounts funds itself very cheaply. One relying on rate-shopping depositors and wholesale markets pays considerably more — the structural gap between Meridian and Northfield.

ROE

Return on Equity

What the owners earn on their stake. Note the lever hidden in the denominator: thin equity mechanically inflates ROE, so a very high figure can signal aggressive leverage rather than excellence.

ROE = Net income ÷ Equity

ROA

Return on Assets

Earnings per dollar of balance sheet, regardless of how it was funded. Immune to the leverage effect that flatters ROE, which is why the two are read together.

ROA = Net income ÷ Total assets

Efficiency ratio

cost-to-income ratio

Operating cost per dollar of revenue. Despite the name, lower is better — 43% means 43 cents of cost per dollar earned. Branch networks push it up; a lean digital-only lender pushes it down.

Efficiency ratio = Operating expense ÷ Revenue

Opex

Operating expenses

Staff, branches, technology, marketing — everything except interest paid and credit losses. This is the line cost synergies attack, because it's the line with obvious duplication between two merged firms.

PCL

Provision for Credit Losses

The expense recorded for loans expected to go bad. It's an estimate booked ahead of the actual loss, which makes it the most judgement-laden line in a bank's income statement — and the easiest one to be wrong about.

Interchange

—

The fee the merchant's side pays the card issuer on every purchase, roughly 1.5% of the amount. The key property: it carries no credit risk. You collect it whether or not the customer ever borrows a cent — which is the entire economic case for customers who pay in full.

Interchange = Purchase volume × ~1.5%

Non-interest income

fee income

Revenue that isn't interest: interchange, service charges, wealth management, insurance. Prized because it doesn't consume capital or carry default risk the way lending does.

CAGR

Compound Annual Growth Rate

The single constant yearly rate that would turn the starting value into the ending value. It smooths a lumpy path into one comparable number — and in doing so hides whether growth was steady or wildly erratic.

CAGR = (End ÷ Start)1/years − 1

bps

Basis points

One hundredth of a percentage point. Used because percentages of percentages are ambiguous: "rates rose 0.5%" could mean half a point, or half a percent of the current rate. "50 bps" can only mean one thing.

1 bp = 0.01% · 100 bps = 1 percentage point

FY

Fiscal Year

The company's own twelve-month reporting period, which need not match the calendar. In this case study FY2025 is the calendar year ending 31 December 2025.

03

Credit risk

The measurement of borrowers not paying you back. Nearly all of it reduces to three quantities multiplied together — and to arguments about how each was estimated.

PD

Probability of Default

The chance a borrower stops paying within a stated window. Always attached to a horizon — a 12-month PD and a lifetime PD are different numbers for the same person, and confusing them is a common error.

LGD

Loss Given Default

Of the amount owed when default happens, the fraction never recovered. An 82% LGD means 18 cents back on the dollar. Card lending is unsecured — there is no house to repossess — so LGD sits brutally high, and nearly the whole exposure becomes loss.

LGD = 1 − Recovery rate

EAD

Exposure at Default

How much is actually owed at the moment of default. On a credit card this is neither today's balance nor the full limit — it's whatever the borrower has drawn by then, which is systematically higher than their historical average, because people borrow more as their situation deteriorates.

Central to this case

EL

Expected Loss

Three numbers multiplied. Almost every dispute in credit risk is a dispute about one of the three factors — and because they multiply, an error in any one scales the whole answer.

EL = PD × LGD × EAD

DPD

Days Past Due

How far behind a borrower is. "30+ DPD" means at least a month late; "90+ DPD" means three months, which is the conventional definition of default. The gap between those two buckets is the warning period — the reason a lender's information always arrives late.

NCO

Net Charge-Off

Debt written off as uncollectible, less anything later recovered, as a percentage of the book. This is the loss that actually happened — the reality check against the loss that was predicted.

NCO rate = (Charge-offs − Recoveries) ÷ Average receivables

Allowance

loss reserve, provision balance

Money already set aside on the balance sheet against losses not yet realised. PCL is the expense that builds it up; charge-offs draw it down. A reserve that isn't growing as fast as delinquency is a reserve falling behind.

Delinquency

—

Being behind on payments. Watched obsessively because it leads: today's delinquency becomes next year's charge-off, so a rising delinquency curve is a loss forecast you can already read.

Adverse selection

—

The people most eager to take what you're offering are disproportionately the ones you'd least want to give it to. A borrower who immediately draws on a new credit limit is revealing an unmet need for cash — and they reveal it only after you've granted the line.

Central to this case

EAD migration

exposure drift, drawdown at default

The pattern where borrowers steadily consume their available credit as they approach default — so exposure is largest at precisely the moment it's most likely to be lost. It's why an unused credit line is never truly free: the bank's right to cancel is worth less than it appears, because the information justifying cancellation arrives after the drawdown.

Central to this case

Bust-out fraud

—

Behave impeccably for six to twelve months, earn a limit increase, draw everything at once across every card, disappear. Engineered specifically around limit-increase programs, because those programs reward exactly the clean payment history a patient fraudster can manufacture.

Thin file

—

A short credit history. Not bad — unmeasured. There isn't enough observed behaviour to model with confidence, and twelve months contains no economic downturn, so nothing has yet been tested under stress.

Behavioural scorecard

—

A model scoring existing customers on what they've actually done since opening the account — payment ratio, utilisation trend, cash-advance use — as distinct from the origination scorecard used when they first applied. Whether someone pays in full or pays the minimum is the single strongest signal it reads.

Credit bureau

—

The agency holding a borrower's credit history across all lenders. A soft pull checks the file without affecting the borrower's score; a hard pull is recorded and lowers it slightly. Bureau data refreshes monthly — one of the lags that makes lender information stale.

Reserve cushion

allowance coverage ÷ charge-off rate

Roughly how many years of today's losses the existing reserve would cover. Northfield's fell from 1.39x to 1.01x across three years — meaning reserves that once carried a comfortable margin now barely cover one year of current losses, with the book still growing at 24% a year.

Cushion = (Allowance ÷ Gross receivables) ÷ NCO rate

04

Capital & regulation

How much loss a bank can absorb before failing, and the rules that govern the answer. Capital is the constraint that decides whether a deal is even possible.

CET1

Common Equity Tier 1

The highest-quality capital: common shares plus retained earnings, less deductions such as goodwill. This is the money that absorbs losses first and without argument, which is why regulators care about it above all else.

CET1 ratio

—

The headline solvency measure. Northfield's fall from 13.5% to 11.5% over three years is the whole risk story in one number — it grew faster than it could generate capital to support the growth.

CET1 ratio = CET1 capital ÷ RWA

RWA

Risk-Weighted Assets

Assets rescaled by how risky they are before being compared against capital — so that $100 of government bonds and $100 of credit-card debt don't count the same. It's the denominator that makes capital ratios comparable across very different balance sheets.

RWA = Σ (Exposure × Risk weight)

Risk weight

—

The scaling percentage itself. In this case: 45% for balances belonging to customers who pay in full, 75% for those who carry debt. The same dollar of receivable consumes nearly twice the capital depending on the borrower's habit — so a book's mix matters as much as its size.

CCF

Credit Conversion Factor

The fraction of an undrawn commitment that must be treated as though already lent, for capital purposes. Cancellable card lines moved from 0% to 10% under the Basel III finalisation — an acknowledgement that unused credit is not risk-free, since it gets used precisely when things go wrong.

Capital-relevant exposure = Drawn + (Undrawn × CCF)

UCC

Unconditionally Cancellable Commitment

A credit line the bank may withdraw at any time, for any reason. It attracts little capital because of that right — but the right is worth less than it looks, since the bank learns it should cancel only after the borrower has already drawn.

Leverage ratio

—

Capital over total assets with no risk weighting whatsoever. A deliberate backstop: it can't be gamed by arguing your assets are safer than they look, so it catches banks whose risk models are too optimistic.

Leverage ratio = Capital ÷ Total assets

Basel III

—

The international rulebook setting how much capital banks must hold, written after the 2008 crisis. "The finalisation" refers to a later set of revisions, which included the change to how undrawn card lines are treated.

IFRS 9

International Financial Reporting Standard 9

The accounting standard for financial instruments, including how loan-loss reserves must be calculated. Its defining principle: reserve for losses you expect, not just losses already visible.

ECL

Expected Credit Loss

The reserve IFRS 9 requires — a probability-weighted loss estimate built from PD, LGD and EAD. For revolving credit it must consider exposure beyond the bank's right to cancel, so granting a limit increase raises the reserve even while the line sits entirely unused.

Stage 1 / 2 / 3

IFRS 9 impairment stages

Stage 1 — performing; reserve covers losses expected in the next 12 months. Stage 2 — credit risk has risen significantly; the reserve jumps to cover losses expected across the loan's entire remaining life. Stage 3 — already credit-impaired, typically 90+ days late.

The step from 1 to 2 is a cliff, not a slope: the reserve can multiply without a single missed payment. It's why a deteriorating book's provision expense accelerates faster than its delinquency rate.

Prudential regulator

—

The supervisor responsible for whether a bank stays solvent — capital, liquidity, risk models, survival.

Conduct regulator

—

The separate supervisor responsible for whether customers are treated fairly: consent, disclosure, affordability. A bank can satisfy its prudential regulator completely and still fail here — which is exactly Northfield's problem, since its limit-increase practices drew an inquiry despite the lending itself being profitable.

Remediation

—

The corrective program run after a regulator finds a problem: fixing the practice, and usually compensating affected customers. In diligence it's a warning sign that outlives its formal closure — the finding is settled, the underlying culture may not be.

05

The card business

Credit cards behave unlike other lending: the customer decides how much to borrow and when, which puts a valuable option on their side of the table.

CLI

Credit Limit Increase

Raising a cardholder's maximum. Unusual among banking products because it costs the bank essentially nothing until it's drawn — and that asymmetry is what makes it both a cheap growth lever and a genuine hazard.

Transactor

—

Pays the balance in full every month. Generates interchange, pays no interest. Low risk, low capital consumption — and barely profitable.

Revolver

—

Carries a balance from month to month and pays interest on it. Where card profit actually comes from, and where the losses live. The central tension of this case: Meridian's book is full of transactors, and Northfield's is full of revolvers.

Revolve rate

—

The share of accounts or balances that carry over rather than being paid off. 40% versus 71% is the difference between a payments business and a lending business.

Utilization

—

Balance divided by credit limit. It does double duty: a risk signal to the lender, and an input to the borrower's own credit score. Hence the irony of a limit increase — it lowers utilisation, improving the customer's score, which makes them a better credit and simultaneously more attractive to competitors.

Utilization = Balance ÷ Credit limit

Headroom

undrawn balance, available credit

Credit granted but not yet borrowed. Invisible on the income statement, very much present in the risk calculation — this is the quantity the CCF exists to capture.

Headroom = Credit limit − Balance

APR

Annual Percentage Rate

The yearly interest rate charged on a carried balance. Notably, it does not change when a limit is raised — the lender adjusts quantity, not price.

Credit rationing

quantity rationing

Lenders limit how much they'll lend rather than raising the price to match risk — because a higher rate disproportionately attracts the borrowers most likely to default, making the book worse rather than better-compensated. So the lever is the size of the limit, not the APR. This is why a limit increase is "priced" in dollars of headroom.

Option

in the financial sense

The right, but not the obligation, to do something. Its value is asymmetric — you exercise it only when it suits you, so it's worth most in the circumstances least convenient for whoever wrote it. A credit line is an option held by the customer; the right to cancel is an option held by the bank. The customer's is worth most exactly when their finances are deteriorating, and the bank's is blunted by the lag in finding out.

Central to this case

Monoline

—

A lender doing essentially one product rather than full-service banking. Efficient and focused — no branch network, low cost ratio — but with no deposit franchise or diversified earnings to fall back on when that one product turns.

Broker deposits

—

Deposits gathered through intermediaries and rate-comparison platforms rather than a branch relationship. Fast to raise and easy to scale, but more expensive and considerably quicker to leave the moment someone else posts a better rate.

Securitization

—

Bundling receivables and selling them to investors as securities, funding the book without needing deposits. It lets a lender grow far faster than its deposit base would allow — which is both the mechanism of Northfield's growth and part of why its funding costs more.

Attrition

churn

Customers leaving. Card acquisition costs run to hundreds of dollars per account fully loaded, so preventing attrition is worth real money — and declining a limit increase is a measurable driver of it.

Wallet share

primacy, top-of-wallet

Being the card someone reaches for first. A higher limit buys this directly: it makes the card viable for large purchases and displaces balances held on competitors' cards.

06

Analysis & method

Terms describing how the numbers in this case were constructed and interrogated, rather than what they measure.

Anchor year

—

The one fixed year from which all other figures are derived. Here FY2025 — every earlier year was built backwards from it so that the case rests on a single consistent foundation.

Back-cast

—

Constructing earlier years consistent with a known anchor and a stated trend — forecasting in reverse. Each back-cast year still has to balance and tie out internally, or the trend is just decoration.

Pro forma

"as if"

Financial statements showing what the combined company would look like had the deal already closed. Always a construction, never an observation — its credibility rests entirely on the assumptions feeding it.

Ties out / balances

—

Internal consistency checks. A balance sheet balances when assets equal liabilities plus equity. A statement ties out when its components sum to the stated total. Failing either means the model is arithmetically broken, whatever the conclusion says.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Weighted average

—

Multiply each item by its weight, sum the products, divide by the total weight. The decision scorecard is exactly this: six dimensions, six weights, one resulting number. The weights encode what the board actually cares about — choosing them is the strategic judgement.

Weighted avg = Σ (valuei × weighti) ÷ Σ weighti

Sensitivity analysis

—

Change one input, hold everything else fixed, and measure how far the answer moves. It identifies which assumptions actually carry the conclusion — and which ones you can stop arguing about.

Stress test

—

Re-running the whole model under a deliberately adverse scenario to see what breaks and whether capital survives. Not a forecast — a structural test. The question isn't whether the recession happens, it's whether you'd still be standing.

Elasticity

—

How responsive one quantity is to a change in another, expressed as a ratio of percentage changes. How much extra balance does a given limit increase actually generate? That number is measured, not assumed.

Elasticity = %Δ in response ÷ %Δ in driver

Champion / challenger

—

Run the current rule (champion) against a variant (challenger) on randomly split populations, and measure which performs better. A controlled experiment. The important implication: these relationships are measured, not derived from theory.

Holdout

control group

The randomly selected group deliberately left untreated, so there's something to compare the treated group against. Without one you can't distinguish the effect of your action from whatever would have happened anyway.

Period-end vs. average

denominator convention

Whether a ratio divides by the closing balance or the average across the year. Both are legitimate and they give different answers — so the convention has to be stated, or two correct analyses will appear to disagree.

Marginal vs. average

—

The cost or return of one more unit, versus the cost or return across everything so far. Decisions are made at the margin: the question is never "is this book profitable?" but "is the next dollar of it profitable?"
